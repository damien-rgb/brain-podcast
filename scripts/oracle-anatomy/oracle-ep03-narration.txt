The Anatomy of an Oracle. Episode three: the answer ladder.

This is the most important episode in the series. If you only listen to one, make it this one, because the idea in it is small, it takes about ninety seconds to explain, and it is the difference between an oracle that produces a story and an oracle that produces a transcript.

Let us start with the naive version, which is where nearly everyone starts, and which nearly works.

You ask a question. You flip a coin. Heads is yes, tails is no.

And that does function. You get answers you didn't author, the gap we talked about in episode one is genuinely open, and you can play a whole session that way. But if you do, you will notice something after about half an hour, and the noticing usually comes with a slight sinking feeling. The game is a bit flat. It is a sequence of facts. And you cannot see why, because the mechanism is working exactly as designed.

Here is why. A yes or a no closes your question, and it closes it completely. It hands you a fact and then it stops. So every roll shuts a door, and you are the only thing in the room capable of opening a new one, which means the entire creative burden has quietly returned to you. Which was the problem you bought the oracle to solve.

The fix is to stop treating the answer as binary and treat it as a ladder with rungs. The canonical version has six, and going from worst to best they are: no and, no, no but, yes but, yes, and yes and.

Now, that looks like it is just a more granular yes and no. It is not, and the difference is the whole point.

Look at what the two halves of those phrases are doing, because they are doing completely different jobs.

The yes or the no answers your question. That is the closing half. But the and, or the but, does not answer anything. It opens something new. It hands you a fresh problem, or a fresh gift, that you did not ask about and now have to deal with.

So a bare yes tells you something. But a yes, but, gives you something to solve.

That is the engine. That is the actual generative act in this entire field. Not the randomness, not the tables, not the emulator. The and, and the but.

Let me run one question through four of those rungs so you can hear the difference, because it is much more obvious in the ear than on paper.

Bruna is at the ford. Is it still passable?

Yes. She crosses. Fine. True, and slightly dead.

Yes, but. She crosses, and the water takes something off her, or she comes out the other side and realises she was watched doing it. Notice what happened: I now have a new situation that I did not think of before I rolled, and I have to respond to it.

No, but. Not here, but there is a fallen tree about half a mile upstream, and that is a different crossing with different problems. The no did not stop the evening. The but redirected it.

And no, and. Not here, and the reason the water is this high is not the rain. Something upstream is holding it, or diverting it, or broke. Which means there is now a thing in my world that was not in my world sixty seconds ago, and it is upstream, and Bruna is going to want to know about it.

Four rungs, one question, four different evenings. And in three of the four, the oracle handed me new material rather than just adjudicating my old material.

Now I want to talk about why this works, because there is a real cognitive mechanism here and understanding it is genuinely useful.

Sit someone in front of a blank page and say: invent a complication. Most people freeze. It is a horrible instruction. The space of possible complications is infinite and there is nothing to push against, and staring into an unbounded space is exactly the condition under which human creativity seizes up.

Now change the instruction. Say: this succeeded. Now tell me how it cost something.

Almost everybody can answer that, and answer it fast. Because it is no longer an open question, it is a puzzle with edges. You have a fixed starting point, the success. You have a fixed requirement, the cost. And your job is only to connect two things that are already given.

The rung is a constraint. And constraints are generative.

This is the single best answer to the objection people raise about all of this, which is: I am not creative enough for solo roleplaying. That objection almost always turns out to be false, and this is why. The people who say it are not short of creativity. They have been trying to work in an unbounded space, which nobody can do. Give them a rung on a ladder and they produce material immediately.

So if you take one practical thing from this episode: do not roll for yes and no. Roll for a rung.

Now some craft, because there are ways to get this wrong.

First, a design rule that trips up a lot of homebrew oracles. The and, or the but, must modify the answer. It must never overrule it. If you roll a yes but, and your interpretation of the but is so severe that the success is destroyed, you have not played a yes but. You have played a no wearing a hat, and you have quietly cheated. The complication attaches to a thing that happened. Bruna crossed the ford. That is now true. The but is a price, not a reversal.

Second, and this is the discipline that everything depends on: you have to accept the result.

This is understandable to get wrong and it is fatal. The moment results become optional, the moment you reroll because you didn't like it, the oracle stops being a box that holds information you don't have. It becomes decoration on a story you are writing, and you are back to episode one's problem with extra steps and a dice tray.

You do not have to accept a result you cannot interpret. That is different, and rerolling a genuinely unusable answer is fine. But rerolling an answer you understood perfectly and simply didn't want, that is the end of the game.

Third, an observation about the no. People worry that a run of negative results will stall the session. And with a flat two-outcome oracle, they are right, it can. Three noes in a row and you are standing in a field with nothing happening.

But look at what the ladder does to that problem. No, but, is structurally an anti-stall. It is a no that is required to hand you a route. And no, and, is the opposite of a stall, it is an escalation, because it makes the situation worse and a worse situation always suggests what to do next. On a six rung ladder, only one of the six outcomes is a plain, flat, nothing-happens no. Five of the six push.

That is not an accident. That is the ladder being designed by people who had experienced the stall.

Now, briefly, how oracles actually produce a ladder mechanically, because the range of solutions is wider than you would guess and it is worth recognising them when you see them.

Some compare two dice against each other, and the size of the gap between them gives you the degree, so a narrow win is a yes but and a wide win is a yes and. Some use a target number and measure how far over or under you landed. Some, like Mythic, roll percentages against a chart and mark the extreme ends of the range as exceptional, so the top and bottom slices of the roll become the and results. Some give you a dedicated second die that only exists to say whether there is a twist and how bad it is.

All different apparatus. All producing the same shape. Once you can see the shape, you can read any oracle on the market in about thirty seconds and know what it is going to feel like.

So to summarise where we are. You need a real question, both of whose answers change something. You need to state the odds before you roll. You need something random in your hand. And you need to read the result as a rung on a ladder, where the yes or no closes your question and the and or but opens a new one.

That is a complete oracle, and it is a good one, and for a lot of people it is genuinely all they will ever need.

But it has one limitation, and it is a big one. Everything we have built so far is purely reactive. It answers what you thought to ask. And a real referee does something more than that. A real referee volunteers.
